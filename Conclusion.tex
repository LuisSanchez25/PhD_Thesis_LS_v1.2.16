\section{Conclusion}

This thesis explored the application of Conscience Self-Organizing Maps (\acrshortpl{csom}) to signal classification and detector characterization in liquid xenon \acrfull{tpc} experiments, with the goal of improving sensitivity for rare-event searches including direct dark matter detection. The overarching motivation was that the multi-dimensional structure of xenon TPC signals cannot be fully captured by the 2D boundary cuts used in existing classification algorithms, and that unsupervised neural networks offer a principled way to address this. By applying \acrshortpl{csom} across two different xenon TPCs — the tonne-scale XENONnT experiment and the small-scale XAMS detector — I demonstrated that this approach consistently outperforms or matches existing classification methods while providing substantially richer information about the data.

\subsection*{XENONnT Peaklet Classification}

In XENONnT, I developed a \acrshort{csom}-aided peaklet classification algorithm to replace the vanilla classification, implemented it into the data processing pipeline, and built the software infrastructure needed for the collaboration to reproduce and extend this work. The \acrshort{csom} was trained on a balanced dataset of 400,000 calibration and background interactions, using waveform area deciles, $\log_{10}$(area), and area fraction top as input features — a feature selection informed by systematic early experiments comparing different input representations.

The resulting classification improved S1 accuracy from 99.08\% to 99.53\% and halved the fraction of misclassified single electrons from 0.30\% to 0.16\%, without introducing any bias in reconstruction efficiency at either the 2-fold or 3-fold tight coincidence level. The reduction in misclassified \acrfullpl{se} directly lowers the \acrfull{ac} background, which was confirmed by generating a pure synthetic AC sample and demonstrating a measurable reduction in AC rate under the SOM-aided classification.

Beyond the improvement in S1/SE classification, the \acrshort{csom} identified 22 distinct clusters grouped into six categories, providing a granular picture of the signals in the detector. Notably, a dedicated gas S1 cluster was identified and validated using thorium calibration data, which exhibited a 14\% higher gas-event rate. Identifying these signals as a distinct cluster enables their rejection before event building, rather than relying on downstream data quality cuts, thereby improving reconstruction efficiency. Additionally, an anomalous concentration of signals on PMT 296 was flagged in cluster 16, warranting further investigation into potential PMT malfunction.

The SOM-aided classification also showed benefits across multiple calibration studies. For $^{241}$AmBe data, 90 additional nuclear recoil events were identified within the NR band without broadening it. For $^{220}$Rn data, 4553 additional events were recovered (+1.05\%), with the improvement concentrated at high energies due to better handling of BiPo interactions from the $^{222}$Rn decay chain. This BiPo improvement is particularly significant for background tagging in high-energy S1-only analyses. The expected largest physics impact of this classification is in the $^8$B solar neutrino search, where the primary background comes from ACs caused by misclassified SEs. Finally, the \acrshort{csom} better identifies $^{37}$Ar S1 signals with high rise-time that were systematically misclassified by the vanilla algorithm due to its 2D boundary, and the subcluster information it provides can inform more targeted data quality cuts in future analyses.

\subsection*{XAMS Signal Characterization}

To demonstrate the generalisability of the approach, I applied a \acrshort{csom} to data from the XAMS experiment — a surface-based R\&D detector with different geometry, electronics, and a novel Hamamatsu R21699 2-inch PMT under evaluation. The \acrshort{csom} was trained on 300,000 calibration interactions from three external gamma sources.

The classification produced 23 distinct clusters compared to the three categories (S1, S2, unknown) of the existing algorithm. The S1 detection efficiency was 97.62\% versus 97.96\% for the current method; however, at the event level the \acrshort{csom} achieved a 7\% increase in event-building efficiency by recovering S2 signals that the current algorithm's hard boundary excludes — 6.9\% of events that failed to reconstruct under the current method were recovered. The finer clustering also revealed populations that the existing algorithm cannot distinguish: double-scatter S2 events; merged S1-S2 signals pointing to limitations of the peak-splitting algorithm; PMT afterpulses (cluster 23); a population of localized S2 signals concentrated in the center of the detector (cluster 11); and channel cross-talk patterns along PMT edges and diagonals (cluster 8). Additionally, a cluster with all detected light coming from the top PMT array and a spatial distribution coinciding with individual PMT channels (cluster 22) was identified — an unexpected topography whose origin remains to be investigated, demonstrating the capacity of the CSOM to surface previously unnoticed detector effects during commissioning.

\subsection*{SOM Methodology and Software Contributions}

To support the SOM work within the collaboration, I developed \textsc{sciSOM}, an open-source Python package providing a maintainable implementation of both the Kohonen SOM and the \acrshort{csom}, along with visualization tools including mU-matrix generation and interactive cluster boundary drawing via \textsc{SOM\_remap}. This package ensures the approach can be reproduced and extended by future collaborators without relying on external environments.

I also developed a novel stopping criterion for SOM training, the Sample Migration (\textsc{SaMi}) criterion, which tracks the migration of data samples between prototype neurons across training steps as a proxy for convergence. This work, presented at ESANN 2024, demonstrated 99.9\% classification accuracy while reducing training time by 50\%, addressing one of the longstanding practical challenges of unsupervised learning — not knowing when to stop training.

\subsection*{Software Infrastructure}

In addition to the classification work, I contributed to the software infrastructure that underpins the XENONnT data analysis. I co-developed and maintained \textsc{xedocs}, the collaboration's metadata management system presented at CHEP 2023. My contributions included testing, identifying and fixing bugs, writing unit tests, and managing the corrections database. \textsc{xedocs} provides a flexible and versioned system for managing detector corrections, with configurable integrity rules that ensure data consistency across science runs. This system provides the infrastructure through which the \acrshort{csom} classification parameters are versioned and tracked alongside all other detector corrections, enabling reproducible deployment of the classification improvements across the collaboration.

I also developed an S3-compatible storage backend for \textsc{strax}, integrating cloud object storage protocols into the data processing framework. This provides a simpler and more widely supported alternative to the current Rucio-based data management for higher-level processed data, and is a step towards the kind of modern, industry-standard infrastructure that future experiments like XLZD may benefit from adopting from the outset.

\subsection{Outlook}

The primary barrier to wider adoption of SOM-based classification remains the cluster identification step. While tools such as the mU-matrix and CONN-matrix substantially aid in drawing initial boundaries, the iterative refinement process — mapping neurons back to physically interpretable parameter spaces and adjusting boundaries accordingly — remains labour intensive. Developing methods to automate or semi-automate this process, possibly by combining the SOM's topological organization with supervised boundary refinement or active learning, would significantly lower the barrier to entry and is a natural direction for future work.

A further limitation of the current approach is its reliance on the existing classification to separate signals from noise before training. Applying the \acrshort{csom} directly to the full, unfiltered data could recover additional signal efficiency at low energies, and may reveal new signal topologies currently obscured by the pre-selection. This would likely require a revised choice of input features to better distinguish low-energy physics signals from noise artefacts.

For even higher classification accuracy, an ensemble approach could be worth exploring: combining the \acrshort{csom} with supervised methods such as \acrshortpl{svm} or Bayesian classifiers in a voting scheme could leverage the complementary strengths of each method, while also providing per-signal class probabilities that could be used directly in likelihood-based analyses.

Future tonne-scale experiments, such as XLZD, can build directly on this work. The \textsc{sciSOM} package and the methodology described throughout provide a ready-to-use starting point for training \acrshortpl{csom} on new TPC datasets, and the lessons learned across XENONnT and XAMS — from feature selection and normalization to cluster boundary strategies and validation studies — serve as a practical guide for applying unsupervised learning to liquid xenon dark matter detectors going forward.
